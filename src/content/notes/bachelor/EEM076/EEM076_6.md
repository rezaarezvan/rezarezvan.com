In this part we'll work in electrical fields, the connection between electrical fields and voltages as well as how we can use electrical fields with capacitors.

### Work
We've briefly gone over work before in this series - but let's consider the work required to *move* charges apart from each other.

For example:

![Two equal charges repelling each other.](./imgs/coulomb_repulsion.svg "fig:coulomb-repulsion")

$$
W = \vec{\mathbf{F}} \cdot d\vec{\mathbf{r}},
$$

$$
\vec{\mathbf{F}}_{12} = k_e \dfrac{q_1 q_2}{r^2} \hat{\mathbf{r}}_{12},
$$

$$
\begin{align*}
W & = \int_{R}^{\infty} \vec{\mathbf{F}} \cdot d\vec{\mathbf{r}} \newline
& = k_e q_1 q_2 \int_{R}^{\infty} \dfrac{dr}{r^2} \newline
& = k_e q_1 q_2 \cdot \dfrac{-1}{r} \Big|_R^{\infty} \newline
& = \boxed{\dfrac{k_e q_1 q_2}{R}}.
\end{align*}
$$

### Work in an electrical field
Now, if we consider a charge moving inside an electrical field:

![A charge moving from A to B in a uniform field.](./imgs/work_in_field.svg "fig:work-in-field")

Let's calculate the work to move from A to B.

$$
W = \vec{\mathbf{F}} \cdot d\vec{\mathbf{r}},
$$

$$
\vec{\mathbf{F}}_E = q \vec{\mathbf{E}},
$$

$$
\begin{align*}
W & = - \int_A^B \vec{\mathbf{F}} \cdot d\vec{\mathbf{r}} \newline
& = - \int_A^B q \vec{\mathbf{E}} \cdot d\vec{\mathbf{r}} \newline
& = -q E_0 \int_A^B dr \newline
& = -q E_0 d.
\end{align*}
$$

Note, in the case when we move perpendicular to the field, the work is **0**.

Another way we can write work is,

$$
\begin{align*}
W & = \Delta U \newline
& = - \int_A^B \vec{\mathbf{F}} \cdot d\vec{\mathbf{r}}.
\end{align*}
$$

This means that the potential is proportional to the charge,

$$
\vec{\mathbf{F}}_E = q \vec{\mathbf{E}}.
$$

If we normalize the work with the charge we'll see a very interesting result,

$$
\dfrac{W}{q} = - \int_A^B \vec{\mathbf{E}} \cdot d\vec{\mathbf{r}}.
$$

If we just interpret this integral, we move a charged particle a distance.

Let's go back to our definition of voltage from the first part - this is precisely the difference in voltage!

$$
[\Delta V] = \dfrac{\mathrm{J}}{\mathrm{C}}
$$

Just as **Kirchhoff's voltage law** tells us that, within a closed loop, the sum of the voltages is 0.

This means that the work done within a closed path,

$$
W = - \oint \vec{\mathbf{F}} \cdot d\vec{\mathbf{r}} = 0.
$$

With all these new formulas, here is a little cheat sheet so that we remember:

![How force, work, field and voltage relate.](./imgs/force_work_cheatsheet.svg "fig:force-work-cheatsheet")

### Electrical fields
If we work backwards from a known voltage - we can find the electrical field!
$$
\begin{align*}
\Delta V & = - \int_A^B \vec{\mathbf{E}} \cdot d\vec{\mathbf{r}}, \newline
dV & = -\vec{\mathbf{E}} \cdot d\vec{\mathbf{r}}, \newline
E & = - \dfrac{dV}{dr}.
\end{align*}
$$

We can define this as the gradient of the voltage,

$$
\vec{\mathbf{E}} = - \nabla V.
$$

### Equipotential curves
As we just discovered - the electrical field is equal to the gradient of the voltage.
Equipotential curves are a way of visualizing the electric potential in a region of space.
They are just like geographical maps!

![Equipotential curves.](./imgs/equipotential_curves.svg "fig:equipotential-curves")

Now the interesting part is that these curves are always perpendicular to the electrical field lines!

### Capacitance
:::definition[Capacitance]
Capacitance is the ability for an electrical potential to *hold a charge*.

We define capacitance as,

$$
C = \dfrac{q}{|\Delta V|}.
$$
:::

#### Capacitors
Capacitors are two isolated plates with **equal** and **opposite** charges: $\pm q$

When they are charged, they create a voltage difference between the plates, naturally.

How can we calculate this voltage?

Assume we have a surface area of $A$, a *surface charge density* of $\sigma$.

We can define charge as,

$$
q = \sigma A.
$$

Let's recall our Gaussian pillbox:
$$
\begin{align*}
\Phi & = \oiint \vec{\mathbf{E}} \cdot d\vec{\mathbf{A}} \newline
& = \dfrac{q_{\text{enc}}}{\varepsilon_0}.
\end{align*}
$$

For a given area, say, $A_1$, we can write:
$$
\begin{align*}
EA_1 & = \dfrac{q}{\varepsilon_0} \newline
& = \dfrac{\sigma A_1}{\varepsilon_0}.
\end{align*}
$$

Which means:
$$
\begin{align*}
E & = \dfrac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0} \newline
& = \boxed{\dfrac{q}{\varepsilon_0 A}}.
\end{align*}
$$

Let's apply this on capacitors,

$$
\Delta V = - \int_+^- \vec{\mathbf{E}} \cdot d\vec{\mathbf{r}} = -Ed,
$$

$$
Ed = \dfrac{q}{\varepsilon_0 A} d.
$$

$$
\begin{align*}
C & = \dfrac{q}{|\Delta V|} \newline
& = \dfrac{q}{\dfrac{q}{\varepsilon_0 A} d} \newline
& = \boxed{\varepsilon_0 \dfrac{A}{d}}.
\end{align*}
$$

This means, the capacitance is **solely** dependent on the geometry of the capacitor!

### Energy stored in capacitors
Recall from earlier that we deduced that,

$$
\dfrac{W}{q} = - \int_A^B \vec{\mathbf{E}} \cdot d\vec{\mathbf{s}} = \Delta V.
$$

This means,

$$
dW = dq \Delta V = dq \dfrac{q}{C},
$$

$$
W = \int dW = \dfrac{1}{C} \int_0^Q q \, dq = \boxed{\dfrac{1}{C} \dfrac{Q^2}{2}}.
$$

How is the (potential) energy stored?

$$
U = \dfrac{1}{C} \dfrac{Q^2}{2} = \dfrac{1}{2} Q |\Delta V| = \dfrac{1}{2} C |\Delta V|^2,
$$

$$
\dfrac{1}{2} C |\Delta V|^2 = \dfrac{1}{2} \dfrac{\varepsilon_0 A}{d} (Ed)^2 = \dfrac{1}{2} \varepsilon_0 E^2 Ad = u \times \text{volume}.
$$

Where $u$ is called the electrical field density,

$$
u = \dfrac{\varepsilon_0 E^2}{2}.
$$
